Crime Patterns in Los Angeles

Introduction

News about crime in Los Angeles often comes in fragments: a story about one neighborhood, one incident, or one dramatic spike in the headlines. It can be hard to tell whether those stories represent the “typical” pattern of crime in the city or just a few extreme cases.

We chose this topic because we wanted to step back from individual headlines and see the broader picture: where crime is concentrated across LAPD areas, when incidents tend to peak across the week and year, and who appears most often in victim records. Using 2023–2024 incident data from the Los Angeles Open Data Portal, our goal is to tell a data-backed story about crime in the city that is easy for a non-technical audience to explore. The visualizations below are designed to help readers move from a citywide overview into specific areas, crime types, and victim age ranges, and then reflect on what those patterns might mean for everyday life and public safety decisions.

Background

Crime reporting in Los Angeles varies across neighborhoods, time, victim characteristics, and incident circumstances. This project focuses on a 2023-2024 subset of the city crime data and uses linked dashboards to help readers compare where incidents are concentrated and how patterns shift once a category is selected.

Data Source

Each row in the dataset represents one reported crime and includes location, time, offense category, victim details, and, in many cases, weapon information.

Guiding Questions (Our Storyline)

We use the dashboards to answer a small set of questions: (1) Where are incidents most concentrated? (2) Does the mix of crime types change by area? (3) Do reports increase or decrease from 2023 to 2024? (4) How do victim-age groups and weapon descriptions vary across incidents?

Looking across the grid, darker cells tend to cluster around certain hours and days—for example, late evening periods on weekends are usually more intense than early weekday mornings. This “temporal fingerprint” of crime becomes even more interesting when you filter by area and crime type: some locations show strong nightlife-related peaks, while others have higher activity during weekday business hours. These patterns connect incident counts to everyday rhythms in the city and suggest when extra attention or resources might have the most impact.

Overall, Dashboard 1 provides viewers with a broader understanding of crime trends in Los Angeles by displaying crime changes over time and by crime type. Instead of just showing the totals, the linked charts allow users to drill down into the data and see how choosing a particular time period or crime category changes the other views. This allows the user to identify more easily which crimes are most common, when they are likely to occur, and whether any patterns emerge by month, day or time. Thus, Dashboard 1 gives the audience a good baseline to understand the key trends in the dataset.

Contextual Visualization

These two supporting charts provide context beyond raw crime totals.

Contextual View 1: Crime Burden Adjusted by Population

This bar chart shows estimated incidents per 100,000 residents for each LAPD area, rather than raw totals. Areas that already looked high in the main dashboard—such as Central or Pacific—still stand out, but some mid‑volume areas move up or down once population is taken into account. This reminds us that a large number of incidents does not always mean a higher per‑person burden; densely populated or highly visited areas will naturally generate more reports. Looking at the chart this way helps separate “busy places” from places where residents may experience a disproportionately high risk.

Contextual View 2: Poverty Rate and Crime Count

This scatter plot examines the intersection of economic vulnerability and crime across Los Angeles. Each bubble represents a specific LAPD area, positioned by its estimated poverty rate (x-axis) and total incident count (y-axis), while the size of the bubble reflects the crime rate per 100,000 residents. The data reveals a clear trend: as poverty rates increase, both the volume of crime and the per-capita intensity (bubble size) tend to rise, highlighting the heavy burden of crime on socio-economically disadvantaged neighborhoods. However, the visualization also captures the influence of urban land use. High-traffic areas like Central and Hollywood appear as outliers sustaining high incident counts despite moderate poverty levels likely due to the influx of daily commuters and tourists. Together, these insights demonstrate that crime in Los Angeles is shaped by a complex interplay of systemic social conditions and local environmental factors.
